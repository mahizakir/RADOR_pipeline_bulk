Cancer metastasis refers to the process by which tumor cells disseminate to distant organs, forming metastatic nodules and invading tissues [[1], [2], [3]]. Metastasis is a major contributing factor to cancer-related deaths [[4], [5], [6]]. Taking breast cancer as an example, metastasis cases account for over 90 % of breast cancer-related deaths, among which more than 70 % are due to lung metastasis [7,8]. Therefore, detecting and treating cancer metastasis at an early stage is crucial [9,10].

A variety of clinical methods is currently available for the detection of cancer metastasis, including fluorescence imaging, tumor marker detection and computed tomography (CT). However, these methods often encounter limitations such as high side effects, limited specificity in targeting and low detection sensitivity [11,12]. With the development of nanotechnology, several nano-drug delivery systems (NDDS) have emerged to improve the physicochemical properties of drugs, thereby enabling more accurate and efficient detection and treatment of cancer metastasis [[13], [14], [15]]. For instance, Almeida and colleagues employed exosomes derived from osteosarcoma cells as natural carriers for 64Cu and indocyanine green (ICG), which were capable of specifically accumulating in lung metastatic lesions [16]. Sang et al. prepared a thermosensitive liposome to amplify the diagnostic and therapeutic efficacy of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), near-infrared fluorescence imaging and tumor photothermal therapy [17]. Liu et al. synthesized curcumin drug-carrying polymeric micelles (Cur-M) with the aim of enhancing the anti-tumor and anti-metastatic efficacy through passive targeting to breast tumor sites [18]. Through targeted delivery to tumor cells, such research strategies enabled a high accumulation of diagnostic and therapeutic agents at metastatic sites.

Before tumor cells infiltrate into metastatic sites, distant organs need to prepare an environment conducive to the survival and growth of tumor cells, referred to as pre-metastatic niche (PMN) [19,20]. PMN is always saturated with abundant tumorigenic factors and immune cells, including macrophages, myeloid-derived suppressor cells, neutrophils and regulatory T (Treg) cells [21,22]. Among these, neutrophils are the first and most abundantly recruited immune cells into PMN, comprising over 70 % of the total recruited cells [23,24]. Neutrophils facilitate the final formation of metastatic foci by supporting tumor cell proliferation, stimulating angiogenesis and creating an immunosuppressive environment [25,26]. Therefore, compared to therapeutic strategies targeting tumor cells, NDDS targeting neutrophils offers the potential for earlier detection of metastatic foci [27]. Furthermore, by modulating the pro-metastatic mechanisms of neutrophils, an integrated approach to the diagnosis and treatment of tumor metastasis can be achieved [28,29].

Research has demonstrated that under cancer conditions, neutrophils exhibit high expression of the SA-binding receptor, l-selectin on membranes, facilitating the migration of neutrophils to metastatic lesions [30,31]. In this way, SA-modified NDDS can specifically target pro-metastatic neutrophils, minimizing drug impact on normal neutrophils. Abemaciclib (ABE), a CDK4/6 inhibitor that has been approved for clinical use in treating breast cancer, possesses the ability to inhibit Treg cell proliferation and enhance effector T cell activity, hence modulating the immunosuppressive microenvironment shaped by pro-metastatic neutrophils [32,33]. However, the clinical utilization of ABE may lead to undesirable side effects and limited effectiveness [34]. Furthermore, only targeting effector T cells and Treg cells was insufficient for achieving a comprehensive enhancement of the immunosuppressive environment [35]. Therefore, the combination of ABE with Cur was contemplated. Cur has demonstrated the ability to hinder transformation, proliferation and metastasis in tumor cells while inducing apoptotic pathways [36]. The aforementioned effects were caused by the regulation of numerous pathways, including NF-κB [37]. Additionally, Cur has been observed to inhibit the release of tumorigenic factors from pro-metastatic neutrophils, including interleukin 1 (IL-1), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [38], thereby comprehensively improving the immune-suppressive microenvironment of cancer metastatic foci [39].

To validate the aforementioned findings, we developed a liposome platform modified with SA derivative (SA-PG10-C18) for early detection and treatment of cancer metastasis. Liposomes modified with SA-PG10-C18 demonstrated an enhanced binding affinity to l-selectin, a molecule highly expressed on the surface of neutrophils [40]. The separation of detection and therapeutic functions was designed to mitigate unwanted toxicities and to better mimic clinical scenarios. Firstly, ICG, an FDA-approved near-infrared dye for tumor diagnosis, was loaded into SA-modified liposomes (ICG@SAL) to enable early detection of tumor metastasis, and SA-modified ABE and Cur co-loaded liposomes (ABE/Cur@SAL) were prepared to inhibit tumor metastasis. Secondly, the potential of ICG@SAL for early detection of tumor metastasis was investigated through various in vivo and in vivo methods. Finally, in vivo pharmacodynamic experiments were conducted to evaluate the anti-metastatic effects of ABE/Cur@SAL, and the underlying mechanisms were explored. Peripheral Blood Neutrophils (PBNs) were obtained from BALB/c mice using a revised density gradient centrifuge method [41]. The blood of mice was put into tubes with heparin and combined with a solution to precipitate erythrocytes. Erythrocytes were then removed by precipitation for 1 h at a temperature of 25 °C. Subsequently, the resulting liquid containing suspended cells was isolated by adding it to the PBN mixture at an 80 % and 100 % ratio (2:1, v/v). After centrifuging the samples for 15 min at 900 × g, the fluid layer located between the 80 % and 100 % fraction interfaces was gathered and washed thrice with chilled PBS to obtain PBNs. The PBN purity was confirmed by a FITC-labeled anti-Ly6G/Ly6C antibody staining process.

SA-PG10-C18 was synthesized by the reaction between SA and PG10-C18 with EDC/NHS as catalysts [40]. Initially, SA (containing 10 mmol -COOH), PG10-C18 (0.5 mmol), EDC (4 mmol), and NHS (2 mmol) were dissolved completely in anhydrous formamide (7 ml) under an atmosphere of nitrogen. The reaction mixture was mixed at 25 °C for 30 min, after which TEA (4 mmol) was added dropwise into the reaction system and stirred for an additional 8 h. The product was then collected, dialyzed (MWCO = 10 kDa) against distilled water for 24 h, and freeze-dried. The purified SA-PG10-C18 was analyzed by 1H NMR (Avance III 400 MHz, Bruker, Switzerland) in D2O and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR-ATR, Nexus-470, Nicolet) with 64 scans at 2 cm−1 resolution from 4000 to 700 cm−1.

The ABE mesylate was loaded in Cur-loaded liposomes via the pH gradient technique [45] (Fig. S2). The key to the technique is to create a pH gradient across liposomes membrane, promoting the spontaneous transmembrane aggregation of drugs in the external aqueous phase into the internal aqueous phase of liposomes. Briefly, a volume of Na3PO4 solution (400 µl, 500 mM) was added to 1 ml of liposomal Cur suspension to regulate the pH of the external aqueous phase (pH = 7.0), resulting in the formation of a pH gradient liposome upon uniform mixing. ABE mesylate (4 mg/ml, 500 µl) was then added to the liposomes in a 10:1 (w/w) phospholipid-to-drug ratio, and the liposomes were stirred at 60 °C for 20 min. The mixture was cooled in ice water for 2 min, resulting in the final product ABE/Cur@SAL (S100: 10 mg/ml; ABE, Cur: 1 mg/ml).

ICG is a NIR-FI dye that is frequently used to observe the behavior of liposomes in both in vivo and in vivo imaging [46]. ICG-labeled liposomes were prepared in the same way as liposomal Cur, but with a 5 % glucose solution as the hydration medium (S100: 20 mg/ml; ICG: 1 mg, 0.2 mg/ml).

The liposomes’ particle size, polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta potential were determined using the Zetasizer Nano S90 (Malvern Instruments UK Ltd., UK). The morphology of liposomes was observed through a transmission electron microscope (TEM, JM-1200EX, JEOL Ltd, Japan). Carbon-coated grids were used for depositing prepared liposomes, and excess liposomes were eliminated after 5 min. Grids were then cleaned with distilled water, followed by negative staining using a 1.0 % solution of uranyl acetate. After drying, they were visualized using TEM [43].

To assess the encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of liposomes, a 10 mm × 25 mm Sephadex G50 chromatography column was employed to remove unencapsulated ICG, ABE and Cur from the previously prepared liposomes. Methanol rapidly disrupts the integrity of the phospholipid bilayer and fully releases the drug encapsulated in the liposome [47]. After lipid dissolution with methanol, the concentration of encapsulated ICG, ABE and Cur was measured using a UV spectrophotometer (UV-1801, UV/vis Spectrophotometer, Gongyi Yuhua Instrument Co., Ltd., China) at 792 nm (ICG), 277 nm (ABE) and 425 nm (Cur) wavelengths. The EE% was calculated using the following equation.(1)EE%=amountofdrugencapsulatedinliposomesinitialamountofdrugappliedtoprepareliposomes×100%

The load efficiency (LE%) of ICG, ABE and Cur in different liposomes was based on the following equation [48].(2)LE%=WdWt×100%

Where Wd represents the mass of the drug contained in the liposome and Wt represents the total mass of the liposome (including the drug and liposome fractions).

Before conducting in vivo experiments, the drugs were subjected to methodology examination using a UV spectrophotometer. It was ascertained that the doses employed fell within the range of the standard curve and exceeded the limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) (Fig. S3).

In vivo drug-releasing profiles of ICG (1 ml, 0.1 mg/ml), ABE (1 ml, 1 mg/ml) and Cur (1 ml, 1 mg/ml) were evaluated through a dialysis method, utilizing 100 ml of dissolution medium (PBS with 5 % Tween 80, pH 7.4). The experimental procedure satisfied the sink condition [49]. Briefly, different preparations were added to dialysis bags (MWCO = 10 kDa). The bags were then dialyzed using a dissolution medium while being agitated at a temperature of 37 °C. At the 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 24 h time points, 1 ml of the sample was retrieved. The sample was filtered through a 0.22 µm polycarbonate filter and substituted with an equal volume of fresh dissolution medium. Filtration with a 0.22 µm polycarbonate filter improved assay accuracy, removed bacteria and microorganisms and prevented damage to the microplate reader [50]. The concentration of ICG, ABE and Cur released was determined by quantification at 792 nm, 277 nm and 425 nm wavelengths using a microplate reader (800TS, Agilent, USA) [51].

The expression of l-selectin on PBN membranes was analyzed using Western blot assays. PBNs were obtained from both metastatic mice and normal mice, then cultured in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. One h later, cells were lysed on ice for 5 min using a RIPA lysis buffer that contained a protease inhibitor cocktail (FD009, FDbio, Hangzhou, China). Subsequently, the lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The total protein content in the resulting supernatant was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay (Thermo Scientific). Equal concentrations of proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Non-specific protein binding on the membranes was prevented with 10 % skimmed milk. CD62L and β-actin primary antibodies (Biolegend) were used to probe the blots overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, they were incubated by horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (Biolegend) and finally visualized using a MicroChemi 4.2 Gel Imager (DNR, Israel) [52].

The uptake of SA-modified liposomes in PBNs was examined in vivo using ICG@CL and ICG@SAL (1 µg/ml). Analysis was performed using flow cytometry (FCM, Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA) and Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). For FCM, PBNs (1 × 106 cells/ml) from lung metastatic mice were implanted into 24-well plates and incubated in RPMI 1640 medium for 1 h. Subsequently, ICG-labeled liposomes (1 µg/ml) were added and incubated at a temperature of 37 °C for 30 min in the absence of light. PBNs were first pretreated with free SA (10.0 mg/ml) in a competitive inhibition assay, then incubated with ICG@SAL at a temperature of 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were harvested and rinsed with chilled PBS. The mean fluorescence intensity of ICG in PBNs was analyzed with FlowJo software.

For CLSM, PBNs at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well were incubated on coverslips in 6-well plates for 1 h. ICG-labeled liposomes were incubated with PBNs in RPMI 1640 medium at a temperature of 37 °C for 30 min in the absence of light. To prepare for the competitive test, excess SA solution was included to hinder l-selectin binding sites on cell membranes. Subsequently, neutrophils were marked with FITC-Ly6G/Ly6C antibody (250 ng/ml) for 20 min. Finally, cells with 4 % PFA were fixed and rinsed with PBS. The nuclei of cells were stained using DAPI. Visualization of cells was achieved using CLSM [53].

Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 technique [54]. PBNs were cultured on 96-well plates, seeding 1 × 105 cells per well in a FBS-free medium. The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 1 h. Studies have shown that neutrophils, as the fastest immune cells in the body to respond to pro-metastatic signals secreted by in situ tumor cells, only need a few minutes to migrate to the PMN (2–5 min to pass through endothelial cells and about 5 min to pass through the basement membrane) [55]. Therefore, PBNs were exposed to various concentrations (0.1, 1, 5, 10 and 50 µg/ml) of ABE-S, Cur-S, ABE/Cur-S, ABE@CL, Cur@CL, ABE/Cur@CL and ABE/Cur@SAL for 30 min. For the preparation of ABE/Cur-S, please refer to Supplementary Experimental Methods 1.18. After the incubation period, CCK-8 (10 µl) was added to the mixture and incubated for 4 h. Optical density (OD) was then determined at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability (%) was calculated using the following equation.(3)Cellviability(%)=[(ODsample−ODblank)/(ODcontrol−ODblank)]×100%

Quantitative cellular uptake of different formulations by PBNs experiments were performed previously to determine the optimal conditions. As shown in Fig. S4, the loading capacity of PBNs was strongest when the concentrations of ABE and Cur were 10 µg/ml, and the incubation time was 30 min. Therefore, PBNs from metastatic mice (1 × 106 cells/ml) were incubated with ABE-S, Cur-S, ABE/Cur-S, ABE@CL, Cur@CL, ABE/Cur@CL and ABE/Cur@SAL (ABE, Cur, 10 µg/ml) to obtain drug-loaded PBNs for 30 min.

The chemotaxis of PBNs that incorporated internalized formulations was evaluated through Transwell assay. PBNs (1 × 105 cells per well), with or without ABE and Cur formulations, were placed into the upper chamber. RPMI 1640 medium, with or without 10 nM formylmethionine (fMLP), was placed into the lower chambers. Subsequently, cells migrated freely for 1 h from the upper chamber. Cells in the lower chamber were captured with an Observer Z1 microscope (Carl Zeiss AG., Oberkochen, Germany) and numbered using a hemocytometer (Neubauer, MeilunBio, Dalian, China) [32].

PBNs from metastatic mice (1 × 106 cells/ml) were co-cultured with various preparations, including PBS (pH = 7.4), ABE-S, Cur-S, ABE/Cur-S, ABE@CL, Cur@CL, ABE/Cur@CL and ABE/Cur@SAL (ABE, Cur, 10 µg/ml) for 30 min. Subsequently, drug-loaded PBNs were collected and suspended in the conditioned medium of 4T1 cells for 8 h. Then, centrifuged to obtain the released supernatant for further analysis. The results of previous experiments demonstrated that co-culturing PBN with 4T1 cells for 8 h effectively released the drug from liposomes (Fig. S5).

First, the MTT assay was used to assess the inhibitory efficacy of ABE and Cur formulations from drug-loaded PBNs on tumors. The released supernatant mentioned above was added to 4T1 cells (1 × 105 cells per well) pre-inoculated in 96-well plates. After an incubation period of 48 h, 10 µl of DMSO was used in each well. 4 h later, OD values were measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated using Eq. (3).

Moreover, the FCM assay was employed to evaluate the immunomodulation efficacy of ABE and Cur formulations released from drug-loaded PBNs. Splenic lymphocytes from BALB/c mice were obtained using a mouse lymphocyte isolate kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). For the lymphocyte differentiation study, the supernatant released from drug-loaded PBNs was incubated with obtained lymphocytes for 48 h and subsequently analyzed using an FCM assay. In this study, we aimed to develop the SA-modified liposomal platform that could specifically target pro-metastatic neutrophils for the early diagnosis and treatment of cancer metastasis. While ICG had received approval for clinical applications in diagnosis and imaging, it came with limitations such as irreversible rapid degradation, a short half-life in the blood and a lack of specific targeting capacity. To overcome these limitations and advance its application in tumor diagnosis, NDDSs were required [39,63]. Therefore, ICG-labeled liposomes (ICG@CL and ICG@SAL) were prepared to verify their efficacy in detecting lung metastasis at an early stage. Subsequently, co-loaded liposomes (ABE/Cur@CL and ABE/Cur@SAL) were prepared to evaluate their ability to inhibit lung metastasis (Fig. 1A). The characterization of liposomes was conducted, including particle size, PDI, zeta potential and EE%. ICG@CL, ICG@SAL, ABE/Cur@CL and ABE/Cur@SAL possessed particle size of 123.6 ± 2.0 nm (PDI = 0.189 ± 0.002), 125.7 ± 3.9 nm (PDI = 0.185 ± 0.007), 126.3 ± 3.0 nm (PDI = 0.178 ± 0.007) and 129.1 ± 2.7 nm (PDI = 0.186 ± 0.008) (Fig. 1B and C), with a zeta potential of −19.4 ± 0.6 mV, −20.2 ± 0.3 mV, −19.2 ± 0.7 mV and −22.4 ± 0.8 mV, respectively (Fig. 1D). This was due to the elimination of unencapsulated free drug using a Sephadex G50 column prior to formulation characterization. Additionally, the presence of SA-PG10-C18 and mPEG2000-DSPE provided some shielding effect [43], resulting in the formulation exhibiting a weakly negative surface charge. The results of the UV–vis light analysis indicated that the EE% of ICG, ABE and Cur in liposomal formulations all exceeded 80 % (Fig. 1E). The purity of PBNs (approximately 7 × 105 per mouse) obtained from metastatic mice was confirmed to be greater than 90 % according to the FCM data (Fig. 2B), suggesting their suitability for subsequent analysis. Since l-selectin could cause the selective ingestion of SA-modified liposomes and was highly expressed on PBN membranes while lung metastasis took place, the l-selectin expression level on the PBN surface was assessed through Western blot analysis. As shown in Fig. 2D and E, the PBN surface of metastatic mice expressed higher levels of l-selectin compared with normal mice (P < 0.001).

In vivo cellular uptake of ICG-labeled liposomes by PBNs from metastatic mice was studied by using CLSM and FCM. The CLSM analysis revealed a marked increase in fluorescence signals of ICG in PBNs treated with ICG@SAL compared to ICG@CL (Fig. 2F). The FCM quantitative study revealed the fluorescence intensity of ICG@SAL was 2.8 times greater than that of ICG@CL. To further verify that the enhanced uptake was attributed to the specific interaction between SA on the liposome surface to PBNs, an excess SA solution was used to block the cellular uptake as a competitive inhibitor. ICG@SAL was significantly inhibited by endocytosis in the PBN due to the reduction of SA-binding receptors on PBN membranes (Fig. 2G and 2H). Moreover, upon examination of Fig. S11, it was evident that tumor cell targeting was not observed with SA-modified liposomes; instead, a preference for neutrophil targeting was observed. The targeting of the formulation on monocytes, lymphocytes, and neutrophils was further assessed through in vivo immune cell uptake assays, revealing a significantly higher ICG fluorescence intensity in neutrophils compared to monocytes and lymphocytes (Fig. S12). These results indicated that SA could specifically bind to l-selectin receptors on the PBN surface from metastatic mice and effectively enhance the uptake of SA-modified liposomes in PBNs from metastatic mice.

We established a series of concentration gradients (ranging from 0.1 to 50 µg/ml) for ABE-S, Cur-S, ABE/Cur-S, ABE@CL, Cur@CL, ABE/Cur@CL and ABE/Cur@SAL to assess their potential to inhibit PBNs. CCK-8 results were shown in Fig. 2I. ABE@CL, Cur@CL, ABE/Cur@CL and ABE/Cur@SAL exhibited lower toxicity to PBNs compared to ABE-S, Cur-S and ABE/Cur-S. This difference may be attributed to the direct penetration of the drug solution into the cell membrane and subsequent entry into the cell [65], whereas liposomes require fusion or endocytosis to enter the cell [66]. After a 30-min incubation with liposomes, PBNs maintained high cell viability (> 80 %). Therefore, it was evident that drug-loaded liposomes did not inhibit neutrophil vitality through direct cytotoxicity. These findings established the potential use of PBNs as carriers for drug delivery to metastatic sites.

We employed a Transwell model to evaluate PBN migration following various preparations for internalization. Drug-loaded PBNs were placed into the upper chamber of the device, while media containing fMLP was placed into the lower chamber to establish a chemical gradient conducive to PBN migration. Subsequently, we investigated the converging capability of PBNs following incubation with various preparations towards the lower chamber (Fig. 2J and 2K). The migration of ABE@CL/PBNs, Cur@CL/PBNs, ABE/Cur@CL/PBNs and ABE/Cur@SAL/PBNs to the lower chamber was significantly enhanced in the presence of fMLP-containing medium compared to fresh medium. By contrast, ABE-S/PBNs, Cur-S/PBNs and ABE/Cur-S/PBNs had poor migratory capacities. The same results were observed in Transwell experiments using ICG-labeled liposomes (Fig. S13). These findings indicated that the migratory capacity of PBNs was dependent on fMLP, while liposomal formulations had little effect on it.

By assessing the cytotoxic effects of the drugs released from PBNs on 4T1 cells (Fig. 2L), we noted a decline in cell viability of 4T1 cells with increasing drug concentration. This finding indicated that drugs released from PBNs maintained their bioactivity and stopped the growth of tumors, producing an antitumor effect. Previous studies had demonstrated that Cur could inhibit tumor development via different means, one of which was by preventing the activation of NF-κB [67]. Furthermore, at Cur concentrations ranging from 10 to 50 µg/ml, PBNs that had internalized SA-modified Cur-loaded liposomes showed a significantly greater antitumor effect compared to PBNs with internalized unmodified Cur-loaded liposomes (P < 0.01). This might be because PBNs had higher uptake of SA-modified Cur-loaded liposomes. Furthermore, the cytotoxicity of varying concentrations (0.1, 1, 5, 10 and 50 µg/ml) of ABE-S, Cur-S, and ABE/Cur-S on 4T1 cells was assessed, and the combination index (CI) values were calculated. The results indicated that the direct cytotoxic impact of Cur-S on tumor cells was relatively limited. This observation may be attributed to the fact that Cur's antitumor effect was achieved through the modulation of the immune suppressive microenvironment (Fig. S14). However, the combination of ABE and Cur exhibited synergistic antitumor effects, with the most pronounced synergistic effect observed at a dose ratio of 1:1 (Table S5 and Fig. S15).

The ultimate goal of treatment for cancer metastasis was to effectively prolong and improve the quality of survival [75]. Among them, the body weight of mice treated with ABE/Cur@SAL did not change significantly, and some of them showed a steady increase, while the body weight of the negative control group and the group of the other agents showed different degrees of decrease (Fig. 5B). The evaluation of long-term survival spanning a 50-d experimental period revealed that the ABE/Cur@SAL group exhibited the highest survival rate compared to all other groups at the end of the experiment. The results indicated the efficacy of ABE/Cur@SAL in improving the quality of survival in mice (Fig. 5C).

Lung tissues and other organs from all groups were collected and photographed for analysis 21 d later, while the number of metastatic nodules in each lung was simultaneously determined (Fig. 5D). The decision to conduct the experiments within lungs was primarily because the lungs served as a pivotal and emblematic target organ for breast cancer cell metastasis [[76], [77], [78]]. ABE/Cur-S displayed superior anti-metastatic efficacy versus ABE-S or Cur-S, demonstrating that combination therapy with ABE and Cur could improve the anti-metastatic efficacy. ABE@CL, Cur@CL and ABE/Cur@CL showed significantly better anti-metastatic efficacy compared with those observed for ABE-S, Cur-S and ABE/Cur-S. The results revealed that the therapy based on liposomes exhibited strong anti-metastatic efficacy. Notably, ABE/Cur@SAL demonstrated the best anti-metastatic outcome, which represented only 9.0 %, 13.0 %, 13.6 %, 21.9 %, 26.4 %, 29.4 % and 46.0 % of the negative control, ABE-S, Cur-S, ABE/Cur-S, ABE@CL, Cur@CL and ABE/Cur@CL groups, respectively (P < 0.01). Mice lung weight was related to the degree of metastasis [79]. The MI index provided a global measure for assessing both the anti-metastatic efficacy and toxicity. Mice treated with ABE-S, Cur-S, ABE/Cur-S, ABE@CL, Cur@CL, and ABE/Cur@CL had lower mean lung weights than mice treated with only 5 % Glu (1.02 ± 0.05 g). The mice treated with ABE/Cur@SAL exhibited the lowest lung weight (0.12 ± 0.03 g) when compared to all other treatment groups. As could be seen from Fig. 5E, the MI index of mice administrated with ABE/Cur@SAL was considerably greater than other groups. The anti-metastatic efficacy of ABE/Cur@SAL was similarly superior to that of ABE@SAL and Cur@SAL in terms of the number of metastatic nodules (Fig. S20A) and MI index (Fig. S20B). This suggested that ABE/Cur@SAL showed the lowest toxicity and the highest anti-metastatic efficacy.

Metastatic situations were additionally analyzed in H&E-stained lung tissues gathered from mice, which were visualized as dark-stained nuclear clusters. The resulting dark-stained nuclear clusters revealed that metastatic foci were detected in all groups except the ABE/Cur@SAL group. These results suggested the superior anti-metastatic efficacy of ABE/Cur@SAL (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, we conducted a histopathological evaluation of hearts, livers, spleen and kidneys to investigate the safety of different treatments. We observed that the liposome-treated groups maintained normal tissue structures without any evident lesions or damage (Fig. S21). The significant inhibitory effect of ABE/Cur@SAL on cancer lung metastasis was further confirmed by tissue distribution assay (Fig. S22). Overall, these results suggested that the excellent anti-metastatic efficacy of ABE/Cur@SAL was crucial to improve the immune therapy in the mouse experimental lung metastasis model.

To further validate the effectiveness of the combination of ICG@SAL and ABE/Cur@SAL in cancer metastasis, we conducted relevant experiments. The results showed that the ICG@SAL-ABE/Cur@SAL group had the fewest lung metastatic nodules, the highest MI index, and the highest survival rate compared to the other metastasis detection-treatment combination groups. The results confirmed the significant advantages of the ICG@SAL and ABE/Cur@SAL combinations in the early detection and therapeutic intervention of tumor metastasis, thus validating the potential application of activated neutrophil-targeted SA-modified liposomes in cancer metastasis therapy (Fig. S23).

To evaluate the anti-metastatic efficacy mechanism of ABE and Cur preparations, an analysis of effector T cells and Tregs in lung tissues was conducted. Metastasis began with the invasion of adjacent tissues by the primary tumor. In this step, tumor cells faced effector T cells that attempted to eliminate them before they entered the circulation [80]. Concurrently, Tregs might facilitate lung metastasis by suppressing antitumor immune responses and/or directly facilitating the invasion and migration of tumor cells [81]. FCM results (Fig. 6A and B) indicated that ABE/Cur@SAL enhanced the ratio of CD4+ cells from 7.4 % ± 0.4 % to 38.5 % ± 0.9 % (Fig. 6C) and CD8+
T cells from 0.9 % ± 0.1 % to 17.5 % ± 0.5 % (Fig. 6D), representing a remarkable 5.3-fold and 19.4-fold increase over the control group, respectively (both P < 0.001). Compared to the control group, ABE/Cur@SAL significantly reduced the frequency of Tregs from 42.2 % ± 1.2 % to 3.2 % ± 0.6 % (Fig. 6E) (P < 0.001). The ABE/Cur@SAL group exhibited the highest CD4+ and CD8+ ratio and the lowest Treg ratio in comparison to other treatment groups. A decrease in lung infiltrating Tregs and an increase in CD8+/Treg ratio could explain the anti-metastatic activity of each group. Notably, the ABE/Cur@SAL group displayed the highest CD8+/Treg ratio among all the groups, with a value of 15.3 ± 3.7 (Fig. 6F). Metastasis was driven by several critical tumorigenic factors. Firm adhesion of tumor cells to endothelial cells, mediated through IL-1, was a key step in promoting metastasis [82]. TGF-β was known to induce cell-autonomous phenotypic changes that contributed to cancer progression and metastasis, playing a pivotal role in lung colonization by cancer cells. In the context of cancer metastasis [83], HIF-1 promoted various processes such as angiogenesis, invasion and induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which was a critical step in the metastatic journey [84].

Immune cytokine levels of lungs, as measured by ELISA, demonstrated that treatment with ABE/Cur@SAL resulted in the lowest IL-1, HIF-1 and TGF-β levels compared to all other treatment groups. These findings suggested that ABE/Cur@SAL effectively reduced immunosuppression (Fig. 6G). VEGF was overexpressed in the metastasis site, where it promoted the colonization of secondary tumors and changed the overall structure of the PMN [85]. Additionally, cancer stem cells (CSCs) promoted cancer growth and aggression [86]. The combination of ABE and Cur displayed a synergistic effect in modulating the PMN. ABE and Cur were effective in suppressing VEGF expression and eliminating CSCs during the lung metastasis process. This was evident in the substantial reduction in fluorescent signals associated with VEGF and CD44 (a surface marker of CSCs) when treated with ABE/Cur@SAL compared to other treatments (Fig. 6H). These findings indicated the multi-faceted impact of ABE/Cur@SAL in combating lung metastasis by targeting various critical effects of the metastatic process.

Clinical tests commonly employed to detect tumor lung metastasis encompass tumor marker assessments, such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 15-3 (CA15-3), as well as CT examinations and pathological evaluations. Research indicates that elevated levels of CEA and CA15-3 are detected once tumor cells have entered the circulation. However, these markers lack target specificity [87]. Additionally, CT examinations are unable to visualize metastatic foci smaller than 2 mm [[88], [89], [90]], and significant lung metastasis are only observable after the primary tumor in mice has progressed to a certain stage, typically around 14 d [57,91,92]. Pathological examination remains the gold standard for cancer diagnosis [93]; nonetheless, it usually necessitates a biopsy or surgical intervention, which can be traumatic and poses risks to patients [94]. ICG@SAL detects tumor metastasis at an early stage by targeting activated neutrophils, which has the advantages of good target specificity and detection sensitivity compared to the above clinical tools.

Since the lungs were frequently targeted sites of metastasis, it was crucial to deliver therapeutic agents efficiently to trigger a targeted and long-lasting anti-tumor immune response to eradicate lung metastasis [95]. Drawing upon the research conducted by Sui et al., who employed neutrophils as carriers to deliver SA-modified liposomes into tumors for SA-mediated photochemotherapy-induced tumor cell death [96], as well as other relevant studies [97,98], we were proposing a novel therapeutic approach that leverages the impact of lung metastasis from breast cancer on the total body immune system. This method targeted the l-selectin-mediated activation of neutrophils. By utilizing the capability to congregate at the lung metastatic site and release drugs in response to microenvironmental cues, we could turn a disadvantage into an advantage. This resulted in the collaborative effect of ABE and Cur at metastatic foci that produced an anti-metastatic effect and enhanced the microenvironment of immunosuppression.

SA-modified multifunctional liposomal platform for the early detection and inhibition of cancer metastasis was reported in the current study. The unique structure of SA-PG10-C18 efficiently binds to l-selectin and improves liposome targeting and stability. During the process of lung metastasis, neutrophils, recruited and activated by the PMN, highly expressed l-selectin receptors that specifically bind to the SA derivative on the liposomal surface. This enabled pro-metastatic neutrophils to act as “Trojan horses” to carry ICG@SAL and ABE/Cur@SAL and move to metastasis sites. The fluorescence imaging study in mouse spontaneous metastasis models demonstrated that ICG@SAL could sensitively identify the microenvironmental changes in distant organs, thereby discovering lung metastasis approximately 14 d earlier than ICG@CL. Moreover, in mouse experimental lung metastasis models, ABE/Cur@SAL was shown to inhibit the proliferation of Treg cells, increase the number of effector T cells, as well as reduce the release of tumorigenic factors such as IL-1, TGF-β, HIF-1, and VEGF. This effectively reversed the metastatic-promoting microenvironment, thereby suppressing cancer lung metastasis and prolonging the survival time of mice. In conclusion, this study provides a novel strategy for the early diagnosis and treatment of cancer metastasis by using ICG@SAL for early detection and ABE/Cur@SAL for subsequent therapeutic intervention upon early recognition of metastasis. We believe that the pro-metastatic neutrophil targeting drug delivery system holds significant potential for cancer identification and therapy.

This study not only offers a novel strategy for the early diagnosis and treatment of cancer metastasis but also presents a promising approach for targeting neutrophil-related diseases, such as pneumonia, rheumatoid arthritis, thrombosis, and atherosclerosis, by specifically targeting high expression of l-selectin on neutrophils using SA derivatives (e.g., SA-PG10-C18). Furthermore, this research confirms the potential for early detection of tumor metastasis by targeting cells in the PMN, such as cancer-associated fibroblasts, myeloid-derived suppressor cells and tumor-associated macrophages, rather than established cancer clusters at metastatic sites. The development of new materials targeting these cells may facilitate early detection and treatment of tumor metastasis.
