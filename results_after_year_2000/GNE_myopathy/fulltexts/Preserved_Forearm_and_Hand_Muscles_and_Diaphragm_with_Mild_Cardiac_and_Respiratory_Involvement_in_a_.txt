GNE myopathy is characterized by distal-onset myopathy, with the presence of a large number of rimmed vacuoles in muscle tissues due to variants in GNE, the gene encoding the bifunctional enzyme UDP-N-acetylglucosamine 2-epimerase/N-acetylmannosamine kinase (1). Although several unusual cases of upper limb weakness have been reported (2-5), most patients develop lower limb weakness with a preserved quadriceps femoris muscles. The early- to middle-stage clinical features of GNE myopathy have been well described (4,6,7). In the advanced stage of GNE myopathy, all skeletal muscles, apart from the bulbar and respiratory muscles, are completely damaged (1,3). The cardiac function is normal in patients with GNE myopathy (1,8). However, the clinical characteristics of end-stage GNE myopathy remain unclear.

Genotype-phenotype correlations have been observed in patients with GNE myopathy. A younger age at onset and an earlier loss of ambulation have been reported in patients harboring p.V603L homozygous variants in the GNE gene than in those harboring other variants (5,9). A decline in the respiratory function has also been observed with disease progression, especially in p.V603L homozygotes of the GNE gene (5).

We herein report a patient with GNE myopathy and homozygous variants (c.1807G＞C, p.V603L) of the GNE gene. The forearm and hand muscles of the patient were also preserved. Mild cardiac dysfunction was observed 43 years after myopathy onset. Furthermore, respiratory dysfunction was noted, albeit with diaphragmatic preservation.

A 67-year-old Japanese man had developed weakness in the left foot at 24 years old. His parents were cousins, and his elder brother had demonstrated similar clinical manifestations without an exact diagnosis, while his younger brother had been diagnosed with osteogenesis imperfecta. At 30 years old, muscle weakness in the right foot prevented the patient from driving.

Upon admission, the facial and bulbar muscles were unaffected. Muscle atrophy was observed bilaterally in the upper arm, upper limb girdle, and medial and dorsal parts of the thighs. The strength of his truncal, medial, and dorsal thigh and gluteus medialis muscles, according to the Medical Research Council (MRC) criteria, was grade 3. The deltoid, biceps brachii, and tibialis anterior muscles were categorized as grade 4 according to the MRC criteria. Tendon reflexes decreased in the upper and lower extremities. Sensory deficits were not observed. The patient walked with a waddling gait. He was unable to stand on his feet. Electromyography of the biceps brachii, semimembranosus, and anterior tibial muscles revealed myopathic changes. The muscle biopsy results revealed variations in muscle fiber size, a large number of rimmed vacuoles, and adipose tissue infiltration; however, the biopsy site was uncertain owing to insufficient medical records. Although a diagnosis of distal myopathy with rimmed vacuoles (DMRV) was made, a genetic analysis was not performed because variants of GNE had not been identified as the cause of DMRV at the time of his first admission (10).

Six years later, weakness in the hand muscles was observed. The patient was unable to walk at 37 years old. However, no reliable clinical information was available until 67 years old. At that time, he was admitted to our hospital because of COVID-19, and his neurological findings were examined. However, no cognitive decline was observed. Facial and bulbar muscles were intact. He could turn his head because of MRC grade 3 strength of the sternocleidomastoid muscles; however, neck flexion was unavailable at a supine position. His forearm and hand muscles were classified as grade 3 according to the MRC criteria. Other parts of the body, including the truncal part and the upper and lower extremities bilaterally, were severely atrophic with grade 0 muscle strength according to the MRC criteria. Hypotonia of the entire body was apparent, and the patient was unable to sit. Tendon reflexes were absent from the upper and lower extremities. Sensory deficits were unremarkable.

Blood test results revealed no evidence of thrombocytopenia. The serum creatine kinase level was low (12 IU/L; range, 59-248 IU/L). Electrocardiography (ECG) was normal; however, transthoracic echocardiography revealed mild cardiac involvement [ejection fraction (EF), 46.1%; fractional shortening (FS), 22.0%] with no apparent valvular dysfunction (Fig. 1A, B). Pulmonary function tests revealed a low vital capacity [percent predicted vital capacity, 65.7%; percent predicted forced vital capacity (%FVC), 67.5%]. Whole-body computed tomography (CT) demonstrated the replacement of muscle tissue with adipose tissue throughout the body (Fig. 2A-H, 3A-D). Several muscles were noticeable in the forearms and hands (Fig. 2E-H, 3D). The triceps brachii and quadriceps femoris muscles were sparsely visible (Fig. 2C, 3A, B). The diaphragm was evident in the images (Fig. 2D).

Homozygous variants of the GNE gene NM_001128227.3: c.1807G＞C (Val603Leu) were detected by whole-exome sequencing of genomic DNA extracted from the peripheral blood of the patient. The variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. These variants were categorized as being “pathogenic” based on the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and Association for Clinical Genomic Science guidelines (9,11).

We describe the case of a patient with GNE myopathy harboring p.V603L homozygous variants in the GNE gene whose forearm muscles and most hand muscles were preserved for 43 years after the onset. The deltoid, triceps brachii, and vastus lateralis muscles were sparsely detected on CT; however, the other skeletal muscles were replaced by adipose tissues. Furthermore, mild cardiac and respiratory dysfunction was noted, although the diaphragm was preserved.

The upper extremities are relatively preserved in patients with advanced-stage GNE myopathy with p.V603L homozygous variants. In 7 out of 44 cases of Korean GNE myopathy, the most frequent variant in the cohort was p.V603L, and the patients were unable to move 8 major joints of the body, including shoulder abduction, elbow flexion, wrist flexion/extension, hip flexion, knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion, neck flexion when supine, and trunk flexion when supine. The disease duration after the onset of these 7 cases ranged from 11 to 41 years (3). The wrist and neck were the last joints that lost their anti-gravity function in this cohort (3), although patients with GNE myopathy harboring homozygous p.V603L variants demonstrated more severe muscle weakness (5,9). In addition, an estimated disease progression model study showed that grip, shoulder abduction, and knee flexion would be spared at 40 years old, which is defined as disease age 0, when knee flexion strength loses half of the power (12). Meanwhile, several patients with GNE myopathy develop upper-limb weakness (2,3,13). In the present patient, parts of the deltoid and triceps brachii muscles were observed bilaterally on CT, although the biceps brachii muscles were almost completely replaced by adipose tissue (Fig. 2B-C). Muscle overuse may be associated with progression of muscle decay in patients with GNE myopathy (2). Although the upper extremities do not follow the distal-to-proximal progression observed in the lower extremities (1), the distal muscles of the upper extremities may be damaged at the latest stage of the disease. Further collection of advanced cases and long-term cohort studies is essential to elucidate the progression of muscle damage in patients with GNE myopathy.

Regarding the lower extremities, the quadriceps femoris muscles, specifically the vastus lateralis muscle, were relatively preserved in the present patient (Fig. 3B, D). The patient developed weakness in the left foot and thigh muscles over a six-year clinical course, which is typical for GNE myopathy (1). An imaging study of the quadriceps femoris muscles in patients with GNE myopathy with a disease duration ranging from 1 to 31 years after disease onset revealed that the rectus femoris muscle was affected first, followed by the vastus lateralis muscle (14). Damage to the quadriceps femoris muscles is important for making the correct clinical diagnosis of GNE myopathy, although severe muscle involvement was observed.

The present case demonstrated mild cardiac involvement with a lower EF (46.1%) and FS (22.0%) on echocardiography (Fig. 1A-B). Although there have been six cases of cardiomyopathy in patients with GNE myopathy, such as two siblings with dilated cardiomyopathy (15), two cases of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (16), and two cases of uncertain type (7), severe heart failure was not reported in 220 Japanese patients with GNE myopathy (5). In this largest registry study, only 3 cases demonstrated a reduced EF of ＜50% (range, 43-49%) (5). Since only one case harboring homozygous variants (p.V603L) in the GNE gene showed reduced EF (5), the cardiac risk in patients with GNE myopathy would be unrelated to the genotype of the patients. Furthermore, a 5-year longitudinal observational history study of 33 patients with GNE myopathy, whose clinical course duration ranged from 3 to 34 years, showed no disease-specific cardiac involvement on ECG, Holter ECG, or echocardiographic examinations (8). In contrast, EF and FS are negatively correlated with disease duration (8). Since cardiac involvement has been reported in a mouse model of GNE myopathy (17), advanced human cases of GNE myopathy may develop cardiac damage. Although the potential cardiac risk is low, regular assessments of cardiac function and further data collection are essential to elucidate the details of cardiac involvement in GNE myopathy.

The present case demonstrated mild respiratory involvement with preservation of the diaphragm (Fig. 2D, 3D). Respiratory failure has also been reported in patients with advanced GNE myopathy (5-7). A prospective Japanese study of 24 patients, among whom 37.5% alleles were p.V603L, revealed that the %FVC decreased after 1 year of observation. Nonambulant patients were more likely to have a lower respiratory function than ambulant patients. Furthermore, two patients harboring p.V603L homozygous variants with marked weakness required non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NPPV) (6). Another report showed that respiratory difficulties were noted in 10.2% (14 out of 137) of cases; however, exact %FVC values were not provided, and NPPV was not applied in any case (7). Thin diaphragm or respiratory muscle damage could be worsened in patients with p.V603L homozygous variants (5); however, the involvement of the truncal muscles, apart from the diaphragm, is related to respiratory dysfunction in patients with GNE myopathy in the long-term clinical course. An imaging analysis of the diaphragm and respiratory muscles is required to elucidate the pathomechanisms underlying a decreased respiratory function in patients with GNE myopathy.

In conclusion, we described a patient with GNE myopathy with homozygous variants (p.V603L) in the GNE gene whose forearm and hand muscles were relatively preserved for 43 years after the onset. The homozygous variants in this patient may be closely related to disease progression. Mild cardiac and respiratory dysfunction develops in the advanced stages of GNE myopathy, with an extra-long-term clinical course, albeit with preservation of the diaphragm.
