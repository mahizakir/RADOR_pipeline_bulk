Limb-Girdle Muscular Dystrophy (LGMD) is a diverse and heterogeneous group of disorders that exhibit autosomal dominant or recessive inheritance patterns. With a prevalence of approximately 2 in every 100,000 individuals, LGMD and its subgroups constitute the fourth most common genetic cause of muscle weakness [1], [2], [3].

Recent advances in understanding the molecular defects underlying these disorders have led to significant changes in their classification. However, the vast heterogeneity of LGMD poses challenges in diagnosis, as many subtypes exhibit overlapping phenotypes. Moreover, the qualitative and quantitative assessment of sarcolemmal proteins has limitations. Therefore, identification of pathogenic mutations remains the gold standard for confirmatory diagnosis [4].

The diagnosis of Limb-Girdle Muscular Dystrophy (LGMD) has undergone a significant paradigm shift, transitioning from labor-intensive and low-throughput methods like Western Blot and immunohistochemistry to a series of technological breakthroughs that have revolutionized the field. The introduction of Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) in the late 1990s has substantially improved the speed, cost, and resolution of diagnosis, enabling the simultaneous analysis of multiple genes and genetic variants. This has led to a better understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying LGMD and its subtypes.

According to the 229th ENMC International Workshop, Limb-Girdle Muscular Dystrophies (LGMDs) with autosomal dominant inheritance, designated as LGMD-D, comprise five well-defined subtypes (LGMD-D1–5), each characterized by distinct genetic and clinical profiles. In contrast, autosomal recessive LGMDs, classified as LGMD-R, encompass a more extensive spectrum of 24 subtypes (LGMD-R1–24), underscoring the remarkable genetic heterogeneity inherent to this disorder [5].

In this study, we aimed to classify patients presenting with progressive muscle weakness or those with pre-existing mutational diagnoses into LGMD subtypes using a cost-effective and efficient diagnostic test that minimizes financial burden on patients. Our investigation explored the genetic and clinical heterogeneity of the phenotypes involved, with a focus on elucidating the complex relationships between genetic mutations, protein dysfunction, and disease pathology. By leveraging the power of NGS and advanced bioinformatic analyses, we sought to refine our understanding of LGMD's molecular underpinnings and improve diagnostic accuracy, ultimately paving the way for personalized therapeutic approaches and improved patient outcomes.

A total of 27 patients presenting with progressive muscle weakness, suspected to have Limb-Girdle Muscular Dystrophy (LGMD), were enrolled in this study from SMS Hospital and the Center for Rare Disease, JK Lone Hospital, Jaipur, India, between April 2021 and April 2022. Informed consent was obtained from all patients and their relatives, and the study protocols were approved by the Institutional Review Board and ethics committees of SMS Medical College and Hospital (Ref No 394MC/EC/2022).

Patients underwent comprehensive evaluations, including clinical history, electrophysiological assessments, histopathological analysis, and genetic testing. They were classified into LGMD-Dominant (D) and Recessive (R) subtypes based on their genetic results. Detailed clinical evaluations included a thorough examination of developmental and family history, physical examination (muscle wasting, hypertrophy, gait analysis, neurological and musculoskeletal assessments), serum creatine kinase (CPK) level, and electromyography. Molecular testing for LGMD was conducted using Next-Generation Sequencing techniques (Whole Exome Sequencing or Targeted gene panel), and muscle biopsy with immunohistochemical staining (α, β, γ, and δ sarcoglycan, Dysferlin, and caveolin) was performed when feasible.

Test results were interpreted in the context of clinical findings, family history, and biochemical data. Patients were categorized into four clinical subtypes: Childhood-onset LGMD (weakness in proximal muscles, onset between 2–18 years), Adult-onset LGMD (weakness in proximal muscles, onset after 18 years), Distoproximal myopathy (weakness starting in distal limb girdle muscles), and Other LGMDs (no/mild weakness with persistent high creatinine kinase levels) [6].

Genomic DNA of patients and their relatives were extracted from blood samples EDTA anti-coagulated blood using a DNeast®, Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen Gmbh, Strasse, Germany). All genomic DNA samples were re-purified and re-eluted in nuclease-free water using the QIAamp DNA micro kit (Qiagen). The quantity and quality of the genomic DNA was determined using both the NanoDrop (Multiskan Sky reader, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. and the Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Rochester, NY). The integrity of genomic DNA was validated on Agarose gel electrophoresis.

Selective capture and sequencing of the protein-coding region of the genomes/genes was performed on targeted gene sequencing. In solution, hybridization of coding exons and flanking intronic regions within the gene was performed on the individual genomic DNA. The libraries were sequenced to mean > 80–100X coverage on the Illumina sequencing platform (Medgenome, Bangalore, India, and LifeCell Diagnostics, Chennai, India). A base is considered to have sufficient coverage at 20X and an exon is considered fully covered if all coding bases plus three nucleotides of flanking sequence on either side are covered at 20X or more. GATK practice framework was used for variant identification. The sequences obtained are aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) using the BWA program [7], [8] and scrutinized using the Picard and GATK Lite toolkit [9], [10] to identify variants relevant to clinical indication.

Analyzed Genes-NGS-based Gene panel comprises of 64 genes (Medgenome, Bangalore, India) of LGMD (Supplementary Table 1).

Sequencing of the protein-coding region in the genes was performed using Illumina next-generation sequencing (LifeCell Diagnostics, Chennai, India) systems at a mean coverage of 80–100X in the target region. Duplicate reads identification and removal, Base quality calibration, and re-alignment of reads based on indels were done using inbuilt Sention modules [11]. Sention’s Haplotypecaller module and Deep variant analysis pipeline on the Google Cloud platform have been used to identify variants that are relevant to clinical Indications.

Gene annotations were performed using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor VEP (Variant Effect Predictor) program of 75 human gene model [12].

We investigated the distribution of disease-causing mutations identified in all cases. The types of mutations encountered in each subtype were analyzed. The exclusion criteria included patients diagnosed with muscular weakness attributed to central nervous system diseases, autoimmune diseases, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, Becker muscular dystrophy, congenital muscular dystrophy (patients who never achieved walking), and those who declined consent for the study.

Data collection sheets were thoroughly reviewed for completeness and securely stored. The data was then entered into Microsoft Excel 2012 and exported to the statistical software SPSS version 17 for analysis. A significance level of p < 0.05 was adopted to determine statistical significance.

Limb-girdle muscular dystrophy (LGMD) is a rare and heterogeneous group of autosomal inherited muscular dystrophies.[13], [14].

In India, the cultural practice of endogamy (marrying within one's community) has led to a limited gene pool within communities over centuries. Although marrying within gotras (ancestral lineage) is prohibited by custom, the tradition of changing a woman's gotra to her husband's after marriage and subsequent marriages of her children with their maternal cousins perpetuates the issues of a decadent gene pool. These practices have contributed to a higher incidence of autosomal recessive LGMDs in India [15].

In this study, Dysferlinopathies (40%) and Calpainopathies (36%) emerged as the most common causes of LGMD. Consistent with previous investigations in India, Dysferlinopathy, GNE myopathy, calpainopathy, and sarcoglycanopathies are prevalent in the country [15], [16]. However, the higher frequency of dysferlinopathy in our study compared to Western data may be attributed to the widespread practice of consanguineous and communal marriages in India [17]. Furthermore, the recurrence of founder mutations in small communities due to isolated genetic drift and inbreeding has contributed to the high incidence of private mutations, which is a major causative factor [17].

Our study revealed a significantly high frequency of founder alleles, consistent with Khalidkar's observation that intercommunal exogamy among the Agarwals led to the emergence of "Agarwal founder mutations" (intron 18/exon 19 c.2051–1 G>T and exon 22 c .2338 G>C) [16]. The high detection rate of founder mutations suggests that targeting these two alleles can lead to substantial detection rates in patients with LGMD phenotype in Northern India.

Correlating clinical phenotypes with genotypes, we found that 6 and 7 cases of childhood-onset LGMD were molecularly diagnosed through genetic testing and IHC, respectively. Among adult-onset and distoproximal LGMD cases, all 7 and 4/5 cases were genetically detected, respectively. LGMD R2 was the most frequent phenotype in adult-onset and distoproximal LGMD, while LGMD R1 was most common in childhood-onset phenotype (Table 1). The age of onset for total LGMD ranged from 10–30 years, with a overall male predominance (n = 17, 62.9%). Notably, childhood-onset LGMD showed faster and more disabling progression of symptoms compared to adulthood-onset LGMD.

Among the 19 suspected patients, 17 had pathogenic mutations, while 2 had variable mutations of unknown significance. Of the 17 pathogenic mutations, 2 patients had two pathogenic mutations, 6 had one heterozygous pathogenic mutation, and 9 had homozygous mutations.

We successfully diagnosed 74% (20/27) of the total LGMD cases. Immunoblot analysis, used as a screening tool, could further enhance diagnostic efficacy. The c.2051–1 G>T mutation leads to a splice acceptor mutation in intron 18 of the CAPN3 gene, disrupting RNA splicing and resulting in a non-functional or absent protein product [16]. In contrast, the c .2338 G>C mutation is a missense mutation, substituting histidine for aspartic acid at codon 780 (p. Asp780His) in the CAPN3 protein. Although the aspartic acid residue is highly conserved, the replacement results in reduced protein expression and autolytic activity in muscle biopsy samples, likely due to the moderate physicochemical difference between the two residues [18], [19]. Notably, both mutations have been identified as founder mutations in the Agarwal community from Northern India, often occurring in a homozygous state or in combination with other CAPN3 variants.

Human dysferlin, a 237-kDa transmembrane protein, plays a crucial role in calcium-mediated sarcolemma resealing. Its structure consists of an intracellular cytoplasmic N-terminal domain, a transmembrane domain, and a short extracellular domain. The cytosolic domain comprises seven conserved C2 domains (C2A-G), which are characteristic of the ferlin family. The C2A domain is involved in calcium-dependent lipid binding [20] Additionally, dysferlin contains FerA [21]and DysF [22]domains, mutations in which can lead to muscular dystrophies. The FerA domain is a four-helix bundle that interacts with membranes in a calcium-dependent manner [21]. In our study, variants were distributed throughout the DYSF gene, but C2F, DysF, and C2E domains showed a higher frequency of variants (Supplementary table 2). We identified six point mutations (34% missense and 66% nonsense), one frameshift mutation, and one intronic mutation, including a novel mutation c. 2212 C>T (p.Q738X) leading to a stop codon. This is consistent with Krahn et al.'s report of 22 distinct novel mutations, including 52% point mutations, 28% frameshift mutations, 18% intronic mutations, and 1% in-frame deletions, with no clustering in specific exons or domains [23].

Western blot and immunohistochemistry (IHC) are essential tools for diagnosing calpainopathy and dysferlinopathy [17]. However, calpain protein cannot be immunostained and requires blotting. While quantitative measurements are useful, they lack specificity due to secondary reductions in dysferlin protein on IHC and calpain on immunoblot, which can also occur in other LGMDs like caveolinopathy and anoctaminopathy [17], [24]. In addition, some gene mutants result in normal or increased protein expression like mutations causing exon skipping or intronic mutations, still preserves the reading frame but the corresponding deleted region is essential for protein expression leading to degradation and deleterious effect, hence, false negative results (17). The cost of blotting is a significant limitation in developing countries like ours, making gene analysis the gold standard for diagnosis [25].

Similarly, the patterns of sarcoglycan expression can predict the primary defect, but the residual sarcoglycan expression can be highly variable, making it challenging to accurately predict the genotype in most cases.

This hospital-based study provides valuable insights into the mutation spectrum of LGMD, enabling more precise management and treatment at our center. Patients diagnosed with LGMD received interdisciplinary care, including physiotherapy, speech therapy, occupational therapy, and deflazacort, with monthly follow-ups. While exercise is widely accepted as beneficial for LGMD, some studies suggest that intense muscle contractions may lead to permanent muscle damage [26]. Ongoing research explores innovative treatments, such as gene transfer therapy, CASPER/Cas9, and exon skipping, to achieve therapeutic goals [27] The prognosis of LGMD depends on the age of onset, severity, and progression of symptoms, highlighting the importance of early diagnosis and intervention.

The major limitations of our study include a small sample size, the lack of immunoblot data for all cases, and limited follow-up data after treatment initiation. Additionally, the WES assay has inherent limitations, including the inability to detect single and multiple exon deletions and duplications, variants in intronic, promoter, and enhancer regions, and areas with large tandem repeats. Furthermore, the assay is not designed to detect mosaicism, which may be investigated at the laboratory's discretion on a case-by-case basis.

Advances in molecular biology have significantly expanded our knowledge of LGMDs, shedding light on their pathophysiology, founder effects of mutations, and phenotype-genotype correlations. As therapies under development are mutation-specific, accurate diagnosis and categorization of these disorders are crucial. Notably, therapeutic trials have shown that multiple muscle biopsies are no longer necessary, as clinical outcome measures can predict disease activity, regression, and treatment efficacy. Dysferlinopathy and Calpainopathy emerged as the most prevalent LGMD subtypes in this study. Further emphasis on understanding pathogenesis, refining diagnostic strategies, and monitoring disease progression is essential to enhance translational research and improve patient outcomes. Comprehensive data collection is vital for informing future clinical trial designs for gene therapies. Therefore, more studies like this one should be encouraged to enhance the quality of life for patients with LGMDs.
